Currencies are the whole game. There are no shares, no companies, no dividends. Just money, priced against other money.

That sounds thin until you realise what it means: **you are always doing two things at once**. Buy euros and you have automatically sold dollars, because the dollars had to come from somewhere. There is no way to do one without the other.

<Analogy>
Imagine a market stall that only swaps things. Hand over your bag of rice and you receive a bag of lentils. You cannot "just buy lentils" at this stall. Every purchase is also a sale.

Forex is that stall, with currencies instead of grain.
</Analogy>

## The eight that matter

There are roughly 180 currencies in the world. In practice, traders spend nearly all their time on eight.

| Symbol | Currency          | Country            | Nickname   |
| ------ | ----------------- | ------------------ | ---------- |
| USD    | US dollar         | United States      | Buck       |
| EUR    | Euro              | Eurozone           | Fiber      |
| JPY    | Japanese yen      | Japan              | Yen        |
| GBP    | British pound     | United Kingdom     | Cable      |
| CHF    | Swiss franc       | Switzerland        | Swissy     |
| CAD    | Canadian dollar   | Canada             | Loonie     |
| AUD    | Australian dollar | Australia          | Aussie     |
| NZD    | New Zealand dollar| New Zealand        | Kiwi       |

These are the **majors**. They belong to large, stable, open economies, which is exactly why the world holds them and why they trade in huge volume. Huge volume means tight prices and easy exits, which is why beginners should stay here.

<Callout>
The three-letter codes are not arbitrary. The first two letters are the country, the third is the currency. US + Dollar becomes USD. Australia + Dollar becomes AUD. Once you see it, you can never unsee it.
</Callout>

Oil is priced in dollars. Most international contracts settle in dollars. Central banks hold dollars as reserves. All of that means the dollar shows up on one side of the overwhelming majority of trades, and it is why so much of this course keeps circling back to it.

## Strong and weak are always relative

You will hear "the dollar is strong today". Strong compared to what? There is no absolute measure. A currency is strong only in the sense that it buys more of some other currency than it did yesterday.

Keep that in mind and a lot of confusing headlines suddenly make sense. "Yen weakens" and "dollar strengthens" can be two descriptions of exactly the same price moving in exactly one direction.
