Retail forex trading is younger than most of the people doing it. That is worth knowing, because it explains a great deal about how the industry behaves.

## Money used to be pegged to metal

For most of history a currency was a claim on something physical. Under the **gold standard**, a country's notes were backed by gold it actually held. Exchange rates barely moved, because each currency was fixed to the same metal.

Two world wars made that impossible to maintain. Wars need spending far beyond anyone's gold.

## Bretton Woods, 1944

With the war ending, the allied nations met at Bretton Woods in New Hampshire and built a compromise:

- Every currency was pegged to the **US dollar**
- The dollar was pegged to **gold**, at $35 an ounce
- Countries could adjust their peg, but only occasionally and with agreement

It worked for about twenty-five years. Exchange rates were nearly static, and forex trading as a business did not exist, because there was nothing to trade.

## 1971: the day the market was born

The United States had printed far more dollars than it held gold for, largely paying for the Vietnam War and domestic spending. Other countries noticed and began asking for their gold.

In August 1971 President Nixon suspended the dollar's convertibility into gold. It was announced as temporary. It was permanent.

<Callout title="The moment forex became a market">
Once the dollar was no longer tied to gold, and everything else was tied to the dollar, the whole structure came apart. By 1973 the major currencies were floating, their values set by supply and demand.

Prices could now move continuously. Which means they could be traded. Every chart in this course exists because of that decision.
</Callout>

## Then it was banks only, for twenty-five years

Floating rates created a market, but it was a market for institutions. Minimum trade sizes ran to millions of dollars, dealing was done over the telephone between people who knew each other, and an individual simply had no way in.

## The late 1990s: the door opens

Two things changed it. The internet made electronic dealing cheap, and brokers realised they could **bundle** small trades: take a thousand customers wanting $1,000 each and pass them to the interbank market as one large order.

That is the entire business model behind retail forex, and it is why leverage, micro lots and free platforms exist at all.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Why the industry feels the way it does">
Retail forex is roughly twenty-five years old. It grew up fast, on the internet, with very little regulation at first. That history is why the space is still crowded with signal sellers, fake gurus and offshore brokers, and why proper regulation arrived late and unevenly.

Knowing the market is young makes it much easier to be appropriately suspicious of it.
</Callout>
