Your platform shows two numbers side by side. Almost every beginner reads them the wrong way round at least once, usually while a trade is open.

The trick is to remember **the words describe what the broker is doing, not what you are doing.**

## Which price applies to you

<Steps
  steps={[
    { title: 'Opening a buy', body: 'You pay the ask, the higher price. The broker is selling to you.' },
    { title: 'Closing that buy', body: 'You receive the bid, the lower price. You are selling back.' },
    { title: 'Opening a sell', body: 'You receive the bid. The broker is buying from you.' },
    { title: 'Closing that sell', body: 'You pay the ask. You are buying back.' },
  ]}
/>

Notice the pattern: whatever you do, you get the worse of the two prices. Every single time. That is not your broker cheating, it is simply what the spread is.

<Analogy>
A used bookshop will buy your book for 100 rupees and sell the same book for 160. Neither number is "the price of the book". They are the shop's two prices, and which one applies depends on which way you are walking through the door.
</Analogy>

## Why charts look like they lie

Your chart is almost certainly drawn using **bid** prices, because that is the convention. But if you are buying, you enter at the ask.

<Callout kind="warning" title="The stop loss surprise">
This catches people out constantly. A short trade gets closed by your broker even though the visible chart candle never quite reached your stop price. Nothing went wrong. The chart shows bid prices, and your short is closed at the ask, which is higher. The ask reached your stop even though the bid did not.
</Callout>

Some platforms let you display both bid and ask lines on the chart. If you trade tight stops, turn it on.

## The mid price is a fiction

When a news site says "the euro is at 1.0850", that is the mid price, halfway between bid and ask. It is a convenient summary and it is not a price you can trade at. Nobody will fill you there.

Useful for reading the news. Useless for planning an entry.
