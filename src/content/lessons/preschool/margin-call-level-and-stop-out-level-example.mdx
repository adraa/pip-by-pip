This is the more common arrangement, and the more forgiving one. Same trade as the last lesson, different broker settings.

## The setup

<Worked title="The account">
Deposit: **$1,000**
Leverage: **100:1**
Broker: margin call at **100%**, stop out at **50%**.

Trade: buy **1 mini lot** of EUR/USD at **1.0850**. One pip is **$1**.
Required margin: **$108.50**
</Worked>

## Two alarms, not one

| Price | Pips against | Equity | Margin level | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1.0850 | 0 | $1,000.00 | 922% | Nothing |
| 1.0550 | -300 | $700.00 | 645% | Nothing |
| 1.0250 | -600 | $400.00 | 369% | Getting thin |
| 0.9959 | -891 | $109.00 | 100% | **Margin call.** Warning, no new trades |
| 0.9905 | -945 | $55.00 | 51% | Almost gone |
| 0.9904 | -946 | $54.25 | 50% | **Stop out.** Position closed |

## The zone between them

Look at the gap: from 0.9959 down to 0.9904. **Fifty-four pips.**

That is your warning zone. Fifty-four pips of market movement during which you are told there is a problem and still have the power to fix it.

<Callout title="What the zone is actually for">
It exists so you can act. Close the trade, close part of it, or add funds. Any of those raises the margin level and ends the emergency.
</Callout>

## What people actually do with it

<Callout kind="warning" title="The honest reason the zone gets wasted">
By the time you are in it, closing means admitting a loss of nearly your entire account. Almost nobody chooses that voluntarily. So they wait, and the choice is taken from them.

The zone is not really a second chance. It is a last chance to do the thing you should have done six hundred pips earlier.
</Callout>

## The point of both examples

Two lessons, two broker setups, and the same conclusion: **a position sized so that a normal market move can reach your stop out is not a trade, it is a countdown.**
