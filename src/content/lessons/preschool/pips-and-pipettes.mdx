Prices crawl. EUR/USD does not jump from 1.08 to 1.09 in a leap; it inches through 1.0851, 1.0852, 1.0853. Traders needed a name for one of those inches, and that name is the **pip**.

<Figure caption="The fourth decimal place is the pip. The fifth, if your broker shows one, is a pipette, worth a tenth as much.">
  <PriceDigits price="1.08505" label="EUR/USD" />
</Figure>

## Counting pips

Once you can see the pip digit, measuring a move is just subtraction.

<Worked title="Two examples">
EUR/USD goes from **1.0850** to **1.0875**.
The difference is 0.0025. Each 0.0001 is one pip, so that is a **25 pip** move.

GBP/USD goes from **1.2640** down to **1.2590**.
The difference is 0.0050, so the price fell **50 pips**.
</Worked>

A quick trick: ignore the decimal point and read the last two digits as the pips. 1.08**50** to 1.08**75** is fifty to seventy-five, twenty-five pips. It works until the third decimal changes, at which point do it properly.

## Yen pairs break the pattern

Japanese yen pairs are quoted with far fewer decimals, because one dollar is worth about 150 yen rather than about 1 euro.

<Figure caption="In yen pairs the pip is the second decimal place. Everything else works the same.">
  <PriceDigits price="150.255" jpy label="USD/JPY" />
</Figure>

USD/JPY moving from 150.25 to 150.75 is a **50 pip** move, exactly like GBP/USD moving from 1.2640 to 1.2690. Same number of pips, different-looking numbers.

<Callout>
Every pair either has the pip at the fourth decimal, or it is a yen pair and has it at the second. There is no third case worth remembering right now.
</Callout>

## Pipettes, and why they exist

Brokers show one extra digit beyond the pip. That is a **pipette**, one tenth of a pip.

It exists because competition made spreads narrower than a whole pip. A broker offering a 0.8 pip spread needs a way to express 0.8. So a price reads 1.08505, where the trailing 5 is half a pip.

You will hear spreads quoted as "one point two pips". Now you know where the decimal comes from.

## What a pip is worth

A pip is a fixed slice of price, not a fixed amount of money. What it is worth to you depends on how much currency you are holding.

<Worked>
On a standard trade size of 100,000 units of a pair quoted in dollars, one pip is worth **$10**.

- Trade 100,000 units, one pip is $10, so a 25 pip win is $250.
- Trade 10,000 units, one pip is $1, so the same 25 pip win is $25.
- Trade 1,000 units, one pip is $0.10, so the same win is $2.50.
</Worked>

The price move was identical in all three. The money was not. That difference is called your trade size, and it is the next lesson.
